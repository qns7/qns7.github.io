My only and favorite guitar amp, modded to perfectly suit myself.

reddit r/GuitarAmps by u/_KJuns originally posted: late 2025

VOX AC15C1 (Revision ISS3c from presumably around 2012) Mods and Repairs/Upgrades

I love this amp. It's been my only amp for a very long time. I made it even better over the years and after doing quite some work recently on it again, I want to share my modding journey of this thing with y'all.

So here are the mods and repairs/upgrades I've done to this thing over the years till today:

Input

  • Made the two channels "jumpable" with an S/DPDT switch, allowing both channels and their input components to be used together.
  • Removed the series input resistor on the normal channel (R1), changed its bright cap from 120pF to 94pF, and made it switchable.

Reverb

  • Changed the stock tank (Belton BS3EB3C1B) to an Accutronics 8EB2C1B and then to a TAD 4EB2C1B.
  • Wired a new shielded RCA cable directly to the PCB connectors and added a classic tone control.
  • Changed R24 from 820k to 330k + B500k to alter the reverb, gain, volume, and noise characteristics.

Tone Stack

  • Changed C20 and C24 to better-quality capacitors with the same values.
  • Added a switchable mids control by changing R32 from 10k to B25k.

Fixed Issues of Later Revisions

  • Grounded the second unused side of U5 (ISS6).
  • Added two caps to V1, presumably for noise reduction (ISS7).

Upgrades and Repairs

  • Changed the screen grid resistors from 470R 1/2W to 1k 2W and elevated them from the PCB.
  • Changed R90 and R91 to higher-wattage resistor arrays and elevated them from the PCB.
  • Changed R79, the cathode bias resistor, from 120R 5W to 143R using seven 1k 2W modern metal-oxide resistors.
  • After measuring before and after, I landed at about 12W plate dissipation per tube and the amp sounds pretty much the same.

Miscellaneous

  • Changed the speaker cable, added tube cages to V2 and V3, added power-tube dampeners, and improved the lead dressing.
  • Experimented with different preamp and power tubes, eventually landing on a 12AY7 in V1, a 12AT7 in V3, and matched EH EL84s.

Outside

  • Removed unnecessary machine screws, added a handle to the back plate, and added a wooden beam blocker.
  • Removed one corner cap so my Captor X can sit there nicely.

Future Plans

  • Try a physical Celestion Blue speaker, improve reliability further, and experiment with the Tone Cut, Reverb Level, and phase-inverter capacitors.

A discussion of the infamous nut slotting gauge, and how I made mine useful for measuring relief and string action. I think it fits into this section of the Qorner, it's a maintenance tool and not "room specific gear" per se.. I guess..

reddit r/Luthier by u/_KJuns originally posted: 2026-08-17

Discussion of the infamous Nut Slotting Gauge

I recently bought a "Nut Slotting Gauge" after wanting one for quite some time. I got mine from Elmer Guitar and paid about 75€ in total, delivered to Germany.

It is meant for measuring nut slot heights, for which it works pretty much perfectly out of the box, regardless of whether you use the 3rd fret capo method or the open string method. Elmer already removed the internal down-pulling spring, leaving only the gravity and weight of the plunger assembly pushing down onto the strings.

The Problem with the Stock Form

The plunger assembly weighs about 14.7g. That turned out to be too heavy for measuring neck relief and string action, because the deflection becomes greater than the actual measurement.

  • High e (.010"): about 0.32mm sag.
  • B string (.013"): about 0.34mm sag.
  • G string (.017"): about 0.32mm sag.
  • D string (.026" wound): about 0.29mm sag.
  • A string (.036" wound): about 0.27mm sag.
  • Low E (.046" wound): about 0.30mm sag.

If the real action on the high e is 1.50mm, the gauge would read 1.82mm. Since every string has a different tension and gauge, the offset changes across all six strings. That defeats much of the point of using a digital indicator, and makes stock-form neck-relief measurements impractical.

The Solution / Mod

I added an upward-pushing spring inside the housing, secured and guided by a chopped-off nail and epoxy. My current favorite is a spring from a Gateron Pro Brown switch, about 20.5mm long with roughly 50g bottom-out force.

  • O-rings and clearance: added between the housing screws and display unit to prevent a scratchy plunger movement.
  • Stop-block: added a small wooden block so the plunger would not get caught on the upper O-ring.
  • Reversible: all modifications can be undone without damaging the gauge.

It works remarkably well. I have about 2.1mm of travel from the floating point to bottoming out, which is plenty even for a 7.25" radius Strat. I also bought copper shim washers to fine-tune the hovering point. They measure 0.8mm ± 0.01mm rather than 1mm.

How My Workflow Works Now

With the washers adjusted so the high e sits only 0.1mm to 0.3mm above the floating point, the net downward load drops below 1.5g to 2g. String deflection on the high e becomes negligible at about 0.03mm.

Thicker strings move the plunger higher and decompress the spring, adding some net weight. In practice, the low E initially showed around 0.1mm of deflection. Adding a third 0.8mm washer restored the counter-push and reduced this to about 0.01mm.

  • Plain strings: two washers, targeting 0.20mm–0.25mm of spring decompression.
  • Wound strings: three washers, targeting 0.30mm–0.35mm of spring decompression.

This makes the gauge practically 1:1 accurate across all six strings for a 10–46 set, with tiny adjustments allowing it to adapt to other guitars.

Conclusion

All in all, I'm quite happy with how this turned out and wanted to share my experience. I may experiment with longer, lighter springs to flatten the force curve further, although the spring cannot be so long or soft that the necessary travel range is lost.

For context, the original StewMac gauge uses a much lighter plunger assembly than this import unit. The difference in downward force is demonstrated in Paul's Milehouse Studios comparison video.
